0. Before we start — the three jobs of a SOC

You already know a SOC is the team that watches a company's computers. This page explains the three main jobs that team does, one by one.

In one line A SOC does three things: (1) collects and joins the clues (logs), (2) learns who the bad guys are (threat intelligence), and (3) notices an attack and stops it (detection & response).

Think of your school again

Job 1 — Log Collection & Correlation: every classroom has a diary. Somebody must gather all the diaries into one room, read them together, and notice the pattern: "the same student's password was rejected 20 times, and then the exam hall door opened."

Job 2 — Threat Intelligence: the police station keeps a list of known thieves — their photos, their tricks, the areas they work in. Your school gets that list and tells the watchman: "these three people are not allowed inside."

Job 3 — Incident Detection & Response: the moment something really goes wrong — a fight, a theft, a fire — the alarm rings and a fixed plan runs: stop it, fix it, check the damage, and make sure it never happens again.

Job 1 — Clues

Logs & Correlation

Collect the records written by every computer, make them the same, and join them into one story.

Like: reading all the class diaries together instead of one at a time.

Job 2 — Knowledge

Threat Intelligence

Keep a list of known attackers, bad websites and their tricks, so you know what to look for.

Like: the police list of known thieves handed to the school watchman.

Job 3 — Action

Detection & Response

Spot the attack early, then follow a fixed plan to stop it, clean up, and write down what to change.

Like: the fire alarm plan: get out, call the fire brigade, then check why it started.

How to use this page Read section 1, then 2, then 3. Section 4 puts all three together in one short story. At the end there is a word list, questions with answers, and a quiz to check yourself.

1. Log Collection & Correlation

This is the raw material of all security work. Without good logs, a SOC is blind. We start with what a log is, then how logs are gathered, then the big idea: correlation.

In one line Log collection means pulling the small records written by every computer into one place. Correlation means joining many small records into one big, meaningful story.

1.1 What is a log?

A log is a line a computer writes down by itself, automatic, without anybody asking: "this happened at this time." Like a diary entry, or a school attendance register.

10:04:12 Ravi LOGIN-SUCCESS 192.168.1.14

10:07:55 Ravi LOGIN-FAILED (wrong password)

Every device writes its own logs: laptops, phones, the Wi-Fi router, the firewall, the website server, the email system, the cloud account, even the CCTV. In a company there can be hundreds of sources and millions of lines per day.

Three things every log must have: When (time), Who (user or device), What (the action). If any one is missing, the log is much less useful.

1.2 Where do logs come from?

SourceWhat it writes downWhy the SOC cares
Operating system (Windows/Linux)Who logged in, which program ran, which file was changedStolen passwords and hidden attacker programs show up here
FirewallWhich connection was allowed, which was blockedShows somebody trying to reach a machine from outside
Antivirus / EDRWhich file was suspicious, what it tried to doDirect evidence of a harmful program
Email serverWho sent what, which links were clickedPhishing mails and fake invoices arrive here
Web server / websiteEvery page request, every form submissionShows hacking attempts against your website
Cloud account (Google, AWS, Microsoft 365)Who created a new key, who shared a file, who logged in from abroadAttackers love cloud accounts — nobody watches them
Wi-Fi / routerWhich device joined the networkA stranger's phone on your office Wi-Fi is a warning sign
Applications (bank app, school portal)Money transferred, marks changed, password resetShows fraud and misuse by people who should be trusted
Golden rule Collect the logs before an attack happens. After the attack, the first thing a clever attacker does is delete his own records. That is why copies are sent to another place, within seconds, where the attacker cannot reach them.

1.3 How the logs travel — the pipeline

Logs do not magically appear in one place. They travel through a pipeline, like post going from many houses to one post office.

1.4 The two ways logs are collected

(a) Push — a small program on each device

A tiny program called an agent is installed on the computer. It watches the log file and sends a copy out to the central place, in seconds.

  • Good: very fast, works even if the device is far away, can also send files and alerts
  • Bad: you must install and update it on every device

(b) Pull — collect over the network

Nothing is installed. The central system reaches out over the network and reads the logs, like borrowing a diary for an hour.

  • Good: nothing to install — handy for routers, cloud services and old devices
  • Bad: slower, some devices refuse to share, and if the network dies you lose time

Big companies use both. A third common method is the device pushing to a middle box (a syslog server or a cloud collector), which then forwards everything onward — so the main system only talks to one box.

1.5 Correlation — the most important word on this page

Correlation in one line Correlation = joining many small, ordinary events into one big, meaningful event by using a rule that a human wrote.

Why one log is never enough

Read these three lines. On their own, each is completely normal:

09:00  Ravi opened his email                     ← normal
09:01  Ravi clicked a link in an email            ← normal
09:02  A file named "Exam2026.xlsx" was copied    ← normal (he always copies it)

Now read them together: a link was clicked, and one second later the only copy of the exam paper left the computer — at 9 in the morning, by a user who is on leave today. Same three lines. Completely different meaning.

A single computer cannot see this. It only knows its own diary. A person reading one line at a time cannot see it either — nobody can hold 4,000 lines in their head. That job belongs to the correlation engine.

1.6 The four kinds of correlation rules

Rule type 1

Counting (threshold)

"If the same thing happens too many times, alarm."

10 wrong passwords in 5 minutes → HIGH alarm

Catches: password guessing, someone scanning your website, a program flooding the server.

Rule type 2

Order (sequence)

"If event A happens, then B, then C — in that order — alarm."

download → change settings → delete logs → alarm

Catches: a real attack following its usual steps, even if each step alone looks harmless.

Rule type 3

Combining (two logs together)

"If a thing happens in two different places at once, alarm."

login from abroad + file copied → alarm

Catches: stolen passwords, because the person is in one city and the login is in another.

Rule type 4

Difference from normal (unusual behaviour)

"This user never does this, but did it today — alarm."

normally 20 files/day → today 4,000 files → alarm

Catches: insider misuse and quiet attackers, with no list of bad guys needed at all. A tool called UEBA does this.

Be careful Every rule must be tested before switching it on. A badly written rule fires on the nightly backup (which always copies 4,000 files) and shouts "ATTACK!" every night at 2 a.m. After a week the tired analyst stops believing any alarm. Too many false alarms is as dangerous as no alarms at all.

1.7 A worked example — three logs, one alarm

LOG 1  (from the laptop)    14:02  Ravi clicked a link in an email
LOG 2  (from the firewall)   14:03  connection to 198.51.100.9  (a known bad website)
LOG 3  (from the file server)14:04  "Answer-Key.pdf" downloaded, then the log was cleared
  1. Collect: three logs from three different devices arrive at the SIEM.
  2. Make same: all three are rewritten with the same labels: time, user, action, device.
  3. Add facts: 198.51.100.9 is on the known-bad list. Ravi's account can open the answer key. The log-clearing action is rare and suspicious.
  4. Correlate: the rule says — email link → bad website within 2 minutes → important file downloaded → log cleared → HIGH alarm.
  5. Alarm: one message, one next step: disconnect the laptop now.

1.8 What usually goes wrong (and the fix)

ProblemWhat happensSimple fix
Silent gapsOne computer quietly stops sending logs. Nobody notices for two weeks — and the attack happened in those two weeks.Send a tiny "I am alive" message every hour. If it stops, raise an alarm.
Clocks not matchingComputer A says 2:00, computer B says 1:40 for the same event. Now you cannot prove what happened first.Set every clock automatically from the same time source (NTP).
Collecting the wrong thingsTen thousand useless messages a day, while the important ones are missing.Write down a list of what you need — and check that list every month.
Logs deleted by the attackerThe attacker erases his trail on the machine he broke into.Copy logs elsewhere within seconds, and raise an alarm if a log is cleared.
Too much noise5,000 alarms a day, only 3 real. The team stops reading them.Tune the rules, switch off the noisy ones, group similar alarms together.
Logs kept for too short a timeYou discover the attack three months later — and the proof is already deleted.Keep them as long as the law or the company requires (often 6–12 months).
Remember this Collection gets the clues. Correlation makes sense of them. Without collection you have nothing to read. Without correlation you have a mountain of nothing. You need both.

2. Threat Intelligence

Now you know how logs are gathered. But a SOC cannot guess every attack — it also needs information about the enemy. That information is called threat intelligence.

In one line Threat intelligence (TI) is useful, checked information about who might attack you, how they do it, and what their tools look like — collected, studied and shared so you can act before they arrive.

2.1 Think of the weather forecast

Every morning the news tells you: "rain is likely in your city today, carry an umbrella, and here are the three areas where water will collect."

Threat intelligence is exactly that, for cyber attacks.

  • Forecast: "Phishing mails pretending to be your bank are spreading this week."
  • Areas that flood: "They target people in the accounts department."
  • Carry an umbrella: "Block these 40 websites and warn your staff today."

Without a forecast you find out it rained after you got wet. Without threat intelligence you find out about an attack after you are hit.

2.2 Two kinds of clues: IoC and TTP

The fingerprints — IoC

Indicators of Compromise

IoC = a small piece of evidence that says "something bad was here." It is the fingerprint, not the criminal.

  • IP address — the network address of a known attacker
  • Domain / URL — a fake login website
  • File hash — the unique "fingerprint number" of a harmful program
  • Email address / subject — the sender of a phishing mail
  • File name and path — where the attacker hides his tool

Easy to use — you simply block the list. But it goes stale fast: an attacker can change his website in a minute.

The habits — TTP

Tactics, Techniques & Procedures

TTP = how the attacker works — his habits and tricks. This is the criminal's style, not his fingerprint.

  • When they work — usually nights and weekends
  • What they do first — send a phishing mail, then move quietly
  • Which tools they like to use
  • Which targets they prefer — schools, banks, hospitals
  • Which mistakes they keep repeating

Harder to use, but it stays useful for years — because a person's habits change slowly. It also catches attacks that use brand new files nobody has ever seen.

A simple way to remember it An IoC answers: "is this exact thing bad?"
A TTP answers: "is this the way a bad guy behaves?"
Police work the same way — a fingerprint is an IoC; "always enters through the back gate at midnight" is a TTP.

2.3 The four levels of threat intelligence

Different people in a company need different kinds of information — like how a student, a teacher and the principal each read a different part of the same report.

LevelThe question it answersWho reads itExample
StrategicWho is likely to attack us, and why?Board / Director"Hackers targeting schools in our country have doubled this year."
OperationalIs an attack being planned against us right now?SOC Manager"A group is talking about attacking exam portals this week."
TacticalHow do the attackers work?SOC Analysts"They always start with a phishing email to the accounts team."
TechnicalExactly what should I block?The tools (SIEM, firewall)40 bad IP addresses, 120 bad links, 3 harmful file hashes.

Levels 3 and 4 go straight into the machines. Levels 1 and 2 are read by people making decisions.

2.4 Where does this information come from?

📡 Open & free

Anyone can read them: Abuse.ch, VirusTotal, AlienVault OTX, government CERT alerts, and lists shared by other companies for free.

Good for beginners and small schools. Can be messy and partly wrong.

💰 Commercial (paid)

Companies whose whole business is researching attackers. They send cleaned, checked, ready-to-use lists every day.

Accurate and fast — but costly.

🏛️ Sharing groups

ISACs — groups where all the banks share warnings with each other, all hospitals with hospitals, all schools with schools.

Best source, because the warning comes from somebody in your own field.

🕵️ Your ownSOC

Your own logs from section 1! If 50 people in your company got the same fake mail today, you have intelligence nobody else has.

Called internal intelligence. It is the most accurate of all.

🕸️ Dark web

Hidden websites where stolen data is sold. Analysts read them to find out if your company's passwords are already for sale.

Dangerous and needs experts.

🧾 Certificates & standards

A common format called STIX and a sharing method called TAXII let different companies exchange intelligence automatically — like a shared news wire.

This is how machines "talk" threat intelligence to each other.

2.5 The life cycle of intelligence

Intelligence is not bought once and forgotten. It goes round in a circle, like a school committee that plans, acts, checks and improves.

2.6 Bad intelligence is worse than none

MistakeWhat goes wrong
Old (stale) dataAn attacker's address changes daily. A list from last year blocks nobody and gives false confidence.
Wrong dataA free list wrongly marks a popular website as dangerous. Your company blocks it and business stops.
No context"IP 203.0.113.7 is bad" means little. "…and it attacked 40 schools this month, pretending to be a teacher" makes somebody act.

How it connects to section 1 Remember enrichment — adding facts to a log? Threat intelligence is the main source of those facts. The log says 198.51.100.9; the intelligence feed says "this address belongs to a group that attacks schools." Suddenly the log means something. Logs + intelligence = a useful alarm.

3. Incident Detection & Response

You have the clues (section 1) and the knowledge about the enemy (section 2). Now comes the moment that matters: something is actually happening — and the SOC has to act.

In one line Detection = noticing that an attack is going on. Response = the fixed plan you follow to stop it, remove it, recover, and make sure it cannot happen again.

3.1 First: is it an alert or an incident?

Alert (alarm)

A warning signal

A message from a machine saying "something odd happened, please look."

Example: "22 wrong passwords in 4 minutes."

Thousands arrive every day. Most are harmless.

Incident

A confirmed security event

A person has checked and decided: "Yes, this is a real attack on our company."

Example: "Phishing succeeded, one laptop is infected, the answer key is gone."

Few, but each one is serious and gets a numbered case file.

Analogy: an alert is the smoke alarm beeping. An incident is the fire brigade confirming there is a real fire. The beeping is not the fire.

3.2 How is an attack detected?

MethodHow it works, simplyCatches
Rule-based detectionA human writes a rule: "10 wrong passwords → alarm." Exact and easy to explain.Known, well-understood attacks
Anomaly detectionThe machine first learns what normal looks like, then alarms on anything unusual. No rule needed.New and strange attacks — but gives more false alarms
Behaviour analysis (UEBA)Studies each user and device: "Ravi has never downloaded 4,000 files before."Insiders, stolen accounts, quiet attackers
Matching threat intelCompares everything you see against the bad-guy lists from section 2.Attacks by already-known criminals
Threat huntingA human goes looking on purpose, instead of waiting for an alarm. Asks "what if they are already inside?"Attacks that produced no alarm at all
Somebody reports itA staff member says "I got a strange mail." Simple, and still the most common first sign.Almost everything — never ignore reports

3.3 Triage — deciding what matters first

When ten alarms arrive together, the analyst must choose. Two things decide the order: how bad it is and how sure we are.

SeverityMeaningReal-life exampleResponse time
Critical (P1)Attack is happening right now, data is being stolen or damagedRansomware starting to lock files across the companyMinutes — stop everything else
High (P2)A strong sign of an attack, not yet confirmedAdministrator password used from another countryWithin 1 hour
Medium (P3)Suspicious, needs checkingA USB drive nobody recognises was plugged inSame day
Low (P4)Probably harmlessOne failed login from a user who later succeededWhen free
The trap Analysts love the exciting cases and ignore the boring ones. Attackers know this — they hide inside the noise on purpose. Boring alarms deserve a look too.

3.4 The response plan — six stages

Fire drills in school follow a written plan, not a guess. Security incidents work the same way. The plan is often remembered as PICERL.

Stage 1 · Preparation

Before anything happens: write the plan, decide who does what, keep backups ready, and practise with a drill. An unplanned response is a panic.

Stage 2 · Detection & Analysis

Confirm it is real, find out what, when, where and who, and decide how serious it is. This is where sections 1 and 2 do their work.

Stage 3 · Containment

Stop it spreading first. Pull the network cable, block the address, disable the account. Quick and slightly rough — because damage is still growing.

Never switch off the computer yet — that erases the evidence you need in stage 4.

Stage 4 · Eradication

Now remove the cause completely: delete the harmful program, close the hole the attacker used, change every password that may have been stolen.

Stage 5 · Recovery

Bring systems back from a clean backup, test that they work, and then watch them extra closely for a few days to be sure the attacker has not returned.

Stage 6 · Lessons Learned

A short meeting with an honest report: what worked, what did not, what we will change. Then actually change it — new rules, new training, better backups. This is how a SOC improves.

3.5 Who does what during an incident

RoleJob during an incident
Incident CommanderThe leader of this case. Gives orders, keeps the timeline, decides when it is over. Does not fix computers — they lead.
Analyst (L1/L2)Confirms the attack, finds the affected machines, blocks the attacker.
Forensics expertCollects and copies evidence carefully so it can even be used in court later.

Communications / LegalTells staff, customers and — if the law requires — the government and police. Never let the technical team handle public statements alone.

Evidence — the rules a 9th grader can remember (1) Do not switch the machine off — that erases memory-based proof.
(2) Do not investigate on the original — work on a copy.
(3) Write down everything you did, with the time — like a chain of signatures (chain of custody). If one signature is missing, a lawyer can throw the proof away.

3.6 How good is your SOC? Two numbers

MTTD

Mean Time To Detect

How long from the attack starting until we noticed.

Good SOC: minutes to hours. Weak SOC: months. Many companies still take 200 days.

MTTR

Mean Time To Respond / Recover

How long from noticing until it is completely fixed.

Both numbers should go down over time. If they stay the same, the team is not improving.

Also useful: how many alarms were raised, how many turned out to be real, and how many incidents repeated (a repeated incident means stage 6 was skipped last time).

The one thing to remember Detection without response is just watching the attack happen. Response without detection is guessing. You need the whole chain: collect → know the enemy → detect → respond → learn.

4. One Story, All Three Together

A short story showing how the three jobs work on the same case, one after another.

The case: a fake exam result mail

Day 1, 09:15. In the accounts department, Meera gets a mail: "Your salary slip — click here." She clicks. Nothing visible happens. She forgets about it.

Day 1, 09:40 — Job 1 (Log Collection & Correlation). Three separate logs arrive: Meera clicked a link (laptop log), her browser reached a brand-new domain (firewall log), and an unknown program started running (EDR log). Alone, none looks serious. The correlation rule says: link click → unknown domain → unknown program, all within 30 minutes → raise a HIGH alarm.

Day 1, 09:42 — Job 2 (Threat Intelligence). The alarm is enriched. The intelligence feed says: this domain was registered two days ago and is already reported by 40 other companies; the program's fingerprint matches a known tool used by a group that steals exam papers. Now the alarm has a name and a face.

Day 1, 09:45 — Job 3 (Detection & Response). The analyst confirms it, marks it a P2 incident, and runs the plan: contain — disconnect Meera's laptop from the network; eradicate — remove the program, reset her password, block the domain everywhere; recover — rebuild the laptop from a clean backup; lessons learned — add a rule that alarms for any link click that leads to a domain younger than 7 days, and train the whole staff next Monday.

MTTD: 27 minutes from the click. MTTR: 3 hours. No data was lost. And next month's attack on the same group will be caught in under a minute, because of stage 6.

6. Questions & Answers

Twenty short questions with simple answers. Use the search box to find one.

What is a log, in one sentence?

A line that a computer writes by itself to record what happened and when — like "09:12 Ravi opened the computer lab."

What is the difference between log collection and correlation?

Collection is gathering the logs from many devices into one safe place. Correlation is joining many of those logs into one meaningful story using a rule. Collection fills the room with diaries; correlation is reading all the diaries together and spotting the pattern.

Why can't one single log prove there is an attack?

Because each single line is usually ordinary: a login, a click, a file opened. Any one of them happens thousands of times a day for perfectly honest reasons. The pattern across several logs and several minutes is what makes it an attack — and only correlation can see that pattern.

What is the difference between pushing and pulling logs?

Push: a small program (agent) on the device sends its logs out on its own, quickly. Pull: the central system reaches out over the network and reads the logs, with nothing installed. Push is faster and more reliable; pull is easier to set up on routers and cloud services. Most big companies use both.

Name the four kinds of correlation rules.
  • Counting (threshold) — too many of the same thing in a short time.
  • Sequence — A then B then C, in that order.
  • Combining — the same thing happening in two different places at once.
  • Anomaly — behaviour that is unusual for this user or device.
Why do clocks on different computers need to match?

Because correlation joins events by time. If one computer is 20 minutes ahead of another, you cannot tell whether the file was downloaded before the password was stolen or after — and that is exactly the difference between an attacker and an accident. All clocks should be set automatically from the same time source (NTP).

What is a "silent gap" in log collection?

When a device quietly stops sending logs and nobody notices. The attack may happen in exactly that window. The fix is a heartbeat: every device sends a tiny "I am alive" message each hour, and the SOC raises an alarm when one is missing.

Why must logs be copied somewhere else?

Two reasons: (1) the attacker can erase the logs on the machine he broke into to hide his trail, and (2) the machine itself can be switched off or destroyed. A copy stored elsewhere within seconds survives both — and "event log cleared" is itself treated as a serious alarm.

What is threat intelligence, using a school example?

It is the list of known troublemakers and their tricks that you hand to your watchman: "these three people are not allowed in, one of them always pretends to be a delivery boy, and thieves are targeting the computer lab this month." For computers: bad addresses, fake websites, file fingerprints and the habits of attacker groups.

What is an IoC, and what is a TTP? Why does the difference matter?

IoC (Indicator of Compromise) is a fingerprint — a bad IP, a fake domain, a harmful file's hash. Easy to block, but goes stale quickly.

TTP (Tactics, Techniques and Procedures) is the attacker's habits — when they work, what they do first, which tools they prefer. Harder to use, but lasts for years and catches attacks that use completely new files.

Where can a student find real, free threat intelligence?

From open sources: Abuse.ch (malware and bad-address lists), VirusTotal (checks whether a file or link is dangerous), AlienVault OTX, and the public alerts of national CERT teams. Real companies also read reports from their industry's ISAC.

What is wrong with old or wrong threat intelligence?

Attackers change addresses and websites daily, so last year's list blocks almost nothing — but it looks like protection, which is worse than having none. Worse, a wrong entry can block a legitimate website and stop the whole company's work. Intelligence must be checked, dated and regularly thrown out.

How does threat intelligence connect to the logs from section 1?

Through enrichment. The log alone says 198.51.100.9 — just a number. The intelligence feed adds: "this address attacked 40 schools this week." Now the alarm has meaning and the analyst knows how urgently to act. Logs without intelligence are blind; intelligence without logs is useless.

What is the difference between an alert and an incident?

An alert is the machine saying "something odd happened" — thousands arrive daily and most are harmless. An incident is what an analyst calls it after checking and confirming that a real attack affected the company. The smoke alarm beeps (alert); the fire brigade confirms a fire (incident).

What are the six stages of incident response?

Preparation → Detection & Analysis → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned (remembered as PICERL). The last one feeds back into preparation, which is why the diagram is a circle.

Why must you contain before you eradicate — and not switch the computer off?

Containment stops the spread while the attack may still be running — that is urgent. Eradication comes after, when you can remove the cause calmly. And you should not power off the machine first, because much of the proof lives in its memory; instead you disconnect it from the network and copy what you need.

What is "lessons learned" and why is it the most important stage?

It is the honest meeting after the incident: how did it start, why did it take so long to notice, what did we do well, what failed. Then you actually change something — a new rule, a new backup, new training. Without it, the exact same attack succeeds again next month.

What is the chain of custody?

A written record of who handled the evidence, when, and why — signatures, times, seals. If one link in the chain is missing, a lawyer can argue the evidence was changed, and it becomes useless in court.

What are MTTD and MTTR?

MTTD = Mean Time To Detect — how long from the attack starting until you noticed. MTTR = Mean Time To Respond/Recover — how long until it was completely fixed. Both should get smaller every month. If MTTD is months, your log collection is broken.

In three sentences, summarise this whole page.

First you collect the logs from every device and join them into one story with correlation rules. Second you use threat intelligence to know who the attackers are and what their tricks look like, so the story gets a meaning. Third you detect the real attack, follow the fixed response plan to stop and remove it, and end with lessons learned so the same attack never works twice.

7. Quick Quiz

Check yourself. Click a question to see the answer.

Q1. In one word, what is the difference between a log and a correlation?

A log is an event. Correlation is the pattern between events.

Q2. Twenty wrong passwords in one minute — is that an attack on its own?

Not on its own, but it is a very strong sign of one — exactly the kind of judgement a correlation rule makes, and then an analyst confirms.

Q3. Name the six steps of the log pipeline.

Sources → Collect → Clean & store → Make them the same → Correlate → One clear alarm.

Q4. Which is better for you right now: 10,000 indicators or 50 checked indicators?

50 checked ones. Quality beats quantity — 10,000 unfiltered indicators drown the machines and the people, and you will not read any of them.

Q5. Your friend says "antivirus and threat intelligence are the same thing." Correct them in one line.

An antivirus compares files against a list of bad ones on one machine; threat intelligence tells the whole SOC about attackers, their addresses, their websites and their habits.

Q6. An analyst gets 5,000 alarms a day. Is the SOC working well?

No. Almost all of them are false alarms, so the team stops reading them and will miss the real one. The rules need tuning — too much noise is itself a failure.

Q7. What must you do first: contain or eradicate?

Contain. Stop the attack spreading before you start cleaning, otherwise it grows while you clean.

Q8. Why is "lessons learned" not just a formality?

Because it is the only stage that changes the rules, the training and the backups. Skip it and the exact same attack succeeds again next month.
