0. Before we start — what is a SOC?

Just two sentences first. The rest of the page builds on this.

In one line A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a team of people who sit in a room (or work remotely) and watch over a computer system all day and night, to spot anybody trying to break in or damage it, and to stop them.

Think of a school

Imagine your school has a watchman at the gate. He stands there all day, watching who comes in. If a stranger tries to force the gate open, the watchman stops him and calls the head teacher.

Now imagine the watchman also watched the library, the computer lab and the office at the same time, and had a camera on every room. That is exactly what a SOC does for computers.

Your school has one watchman and a few rooms. A big company has a SOC that watches hundreds of rooms — hundreds of computers, servers, mobile phones, cloud accounts and websites.

Why a SOC is needed at all

👀 Watch

Every computer writes a record of what happened, all by itself. A SOC reads those records looking for something wrong.

🔎 Find out

If something looks wrong, a person checks it properly and decides: is this a real attack, or a false alarm?

🛑 Stop it

Turn off the account, block the bad computer, save the files, and fix the hole so it cannot happen again.

1. SOC Structure & Roles

A SOC is not one person at one desk. It is a team arranged in levels — like a school has a principal, teachers and a watchman. Each level has a different job.

1.1 The three levels

Here is the easiest way to remember it — compare it to your own school:

Level 1 (L1) — the Watchman

Checks every alarm

Gets all the alarms, one after another.

Does: reads the message, checks the small easy details, throws away the ones that are harmless, and passes the real problems to Level 2.

Like: the watchman who checks every visitor's pass and only calls the teacher if something is really off.

Level 2 (L2) — the Teacher

Investigates the real problem

Gets only the serious cases that L1 could not solve.

Does: digs deep, checks the computer properly, finds out how the attacker got in, and stops the attack.

Like: the teacher who finds out who broke the window and makes sure it is fixed.

Level 3 (L3) — the Principal

Looks for attacks nobody caught

Goes looking for danger on purpose, instead of waiting for alarms.

Does: studies the newest kinds of attacks, teaches the team what to look for, and improves the rules so the same attack is caught faster next time.

Like: the principal who checks why the school keeps having the same problem and fixes the system, not just today's case.

1.2 Who works in a SOC?

Not everyone on the team is a "hacker". Here is the full list of jobs, written simply.

JobWhat they do, in simple words

Shift LeaderIn charge of one shift (one part of the day). Gives work to the team, handles the most serious alarms, and hands over to the next shift.
Level 1 AnalystThe watchman. Reads every alarm, closes the harmless ones, passes on the real ones.
Level 2 Analyst / InvestigatorThe teacher. Solves the hard cases and stops the attack.

Threat Intelligence AnalystStudies known attacker groups and lists of "bad" websites and addresses, so the team knows what to look for.
Malware AnalystStudies the harmful programs attackers send, like a doctor studying a disease, to find a cure or a way to remove it.
Digital Forensics Expert (DFIR)After an attack, carefully collects proof from the computer, like a police detective collecting evidence.

Compliance AnalystMakes sure the SOC's work satisfies the rules written in company law and standards (like ISO or PCI-DSS).

1.3 How is the work divided during the day?

Since attacks can come at 3 in the morning, the SOC must be awake all 24 hours. So the day is cut into shifts, like a hospital or a factory.

Remember this A L1 checks the alarms. A L2 solves the problem. A L3 hunts for the problem that nobody noticed. Above them sits the Manager, and beside them work the people who build the rules, study the attackers, and improve the tools.

2. SIEM Architecture

A SOC has people. But people cannot read the millions of records computers write. So the SOC also needs a tool. The most important tool is the SIEM.

SIEM in one line SIEM = Security Information and Event Management. It is a program that collects the records from every computer in one place, makes them all look the same, looks for patterns, and tells the SOC when it finds something suspicious.

2.1 The school-diary idea

Every computer in a school keeps a small diary called a log. It writes down everything:

09:12 - Ravi opened the computer lab   09:15 - wrong password, 3 times

Now imagine there are 200 computers, each with its own diary, each written in a slightly different style, kept in a locked drawer in each room.

  • If a thief came in, nobody would notice — nobody is reading 200 diaries.
  • Even if somebody did read them, the words are all different in every diary, so you cannot easily look for the same thing in all of them.

The SIEM is the school's master diary. It keeps a copy of all 200 diaries together, rewrites them in one common style, and then reads them looking for patterns.

2.2 How the SIEM works — five simple steps

2.3 Each step explained slowly

Step 1 · Collect the records

The SIEM collects the diaries (logs) from everything:

  • Servers and computers (who logged in, which files were opened)
  • Firewalls and internet connections (which website was blocked)
  • Antivirus / EDR programs (what programs ran)
  • Cloud accounts (who created a new key, who logged in)
  • Web servers, email, mobile phones, and the office Wi-Fi

There are two ways to do it: (a) install a small program on each computer that pushes the records out, or (b) pull them from the internet without installing anything. Big companies use both.

Step 2 · Make them all look the same

This is called normalisation. It is the most important step and the hardest to understand, so here it is slowly.

Computer A says:
"Aug 12 11:03 admin FAILED from 10.2.3.4"

Computer B says:
"FAIL_LOGIN u=admin src=10.2.3.4 t=Aug12 11:03"

Different words, same meaning. The SIEM rewrites both into one standard form with the same labels, so that one search can find both. Without this step, you would need a different search for every single computer.

Step 3 · Add extra information

This is called enrichment. The SIEM adds facts it already knows, so the alarm becomes useful:

  • 10.2.3.4 is the address of a computer in a different city
  • That address is on the "known bad list" of attackers
  • The user admin is the most powerful account in the company
  • That computer is supposed to be in the office, not on the internet

Now the alarm says much more than before: not just "something happened", but "a powerful account is being used by a known attacker from outside, right now."

Step 4 · Look for patterns

This is called correlation — and it is the heart of the whole SOC.

One wrong password is not an attack — people forget passwords. But twenty wrong passwords in one minute is an attack. A computer cannot feel that difference on its own. The SIEM is told the rule:

if the same user fails 10 times in 5 minutes
  → raise one HIGH alarm

That is correlation: many small, ordinary events joining together into one big, meaningful one.

Step 5 · Tell the SOC clearly

Instead of throwing 4,000 raw lines at the analyst, the SIEM sends one tidy message:

🚨 HIGH — Someone is guessing the admin password
  User: admin (most powerful account)
  From: 10.2.3.4 (known attacker, outside office)
  How many: 22 wrong tries in 4 minutes
  What to do: block this address now

That message is called an alarm (alert). The analyst can understand it in ten seconds and act on it.

Where the alarm goes

Once the alarm is raised, this is the chain of events:

  • On a computer screen inside the SOC, in the Level 1 queue
  • A phone call (a "page") for the most serious alarms only
  • An automatic action — the bad address is blocked, the account is locked, a ticket is created (this is done by a partner tool called SOAR)
  • A dashboard so the manager can see how many alarms came today

SIEM vs SOAR: the SIEM is the eye that spots the problem. SOAR is the hand that acts on it.

3. One Simple Example

A short story showing all five steps working together. Read the raw records first, then see how the SIEM turns them into one useful alarm.

Step A — The records as they arrive

These are the raw lines from three different computers. On their own, none of them looks dangerous. That is the whole problem.

🖥️  Computer 1 (the laptop)      02:10  someone emailed Ravi a fake login page
🖥️  Computer 1 (the laptop)      02:14  Ravi's password was used from India
🖥️  Computer 1 (the laptop)      02:15  Ravi's password was used from Germany

🖥️  Computer 2 (the office Wi-Fi) 02:16  password wrong  (1st time)
🖥️  Computer 2 (the office Wi-Fi) 02:16  password wrong  (2nd time)
🖥️  Computer 2 (the office Wi-Fi) 02:16  password wrong  (3rd time)
🖥️  Computer 2 (the office Wi-Fi) 02:17  password wrong  (4th time)
                                ... and 18 more of these ...

🖥️  Computer 3 (the file server)  02:18  file named "Salary2026.xlsx" was opened
The problem A person reading these would think: "Ravi forgot his password." Maybe. Or maybe it does not. Nobody can tell from one line at a time. A diary of single lines is a lie — it hides the story.

Step B — What the SIEM figured out

  1. Step 1 – Collect: all 22 lines came in, from 3 different computers.
  2. Step 2 – Same words: the SIEM rewrote them all as action = "password-failed", user = "rvimala", time, computer — the same labels for every computer.
  3. Step 3 – Add facts: Ravi is in Chennai and always logs in from Chennai. The address used is on the "known attacker" list. The attacker's address is not an office address. Ravi's salary file lives only on Computer 3.
  4. Step 4 – Pattern: the rule was — "20+ wrong passwords for one user in 5 minutes, followed by an important file being opened" → raise a HIGH alarm.
  5. Step 5 – Alarm: one clear message, with the next step already decided.
The one thing to remember Not one of the 22 lines said "attack". They were all ordinary lines. The pattern is the attack. That is exactly what a SOC and its SIEM exist to find.

5. Questions & Answers

Twenty short questions with simple answers. Use the search box to find one.

What is a SOC?

A Security Operations Center is a team of people who watch over a computer system all day and night. They read the records the computers write, look for signs of an attack, and stop it. It is the security control room for computers.

Is a SOC the same thing as an antivirus?

No. An antivirus (or EDR) is a program on one computer that looks for known bad programs. A SOC is a team of people that watches the whole company, and can also catch attacks that no antivirus will ever know about, such as somebody using a stolen password.

Is a SOC different from a NOC?

Yes. A NOC (Network Operations Center) watches whether things are working — "the internet is down", "the printer is slow". A SOC watches whether things are safe — "somebody is breaking in". Many companies run both in the same room.

Why does the SOC work 24×7?

Because attacks do not wait for office hours. Somebody can break in at 3 a.m. and finish their work before anybody arrives at 9 a.m. Attackers even choose nights and weekends, because nobody is watching. That is why the work is split into four shifts.

What is the difference between L1, L2 and L3?
  • L1 — the watchman. Checks every alarm, throws out the harmless ones, passes on the real ones.
  • L2 — the teacher. Solves the hard cases and stops the attack.
  • L3 — the principal. Searches for attacks that gave no alarm, and teaches the team what to look for next time.
Does a SOC manager do the technical work too?

Usually not much. The manager mostly manages — budget, shifts, training, and making sure the serious alarms were handled properly. When a really big attack happens, the manager decides what the company tells the public, the customers and the police.

Does every company need a full SOC?

No — but everyone needs the work to be done. A small company can use a cloud SIEM, switch on about ten ready-made rules, and pay an outside company to watch it 24 hours instead of hiring a full team. Big companies build their own.

What is a SIEM?

Security Information and Event Management. It is the tool that collects the log records from every computer in one place, makes them all the same, looks for patterns, and sends one clear alarm to the SOC when it finds something wrong.

Computers already save logs. Why do we need a SIEM?

Because of three reasons:
(1) Volume — a company makes far more records than any person can read.
(2) Location — each computer keeps its own diary, so you must open 200 computers to see one story.
(3) Different languages — every computer writes in its own style, so you cannot search them all at once.

The SIEM fixes all three: one place, one style, and a machine that reads everything for you.

Is a SIEM a virus scanner?

No. A virus scanner compares files against a list of known bad ones. A SIEM compares behaviour against patterns. It can catch an attack that uses no virus at all — for example, somebody using a stolen password to log in. That kind of attack is actually the most common kind there is.

What does "normalisation" mean?

Making everything look the same. One computer writes "admin FAILED from 10.2.3.4" and another writes "FAIL_LOGIN u=admin src=10.2.3.4". The meaning is identical but the words are not. The SIEM rewrites both into one standard form with the same labels, so a single search finds both. Without this, you would need thousands of different searches.

What does "enrichment" mean?

Adding facts the SIEM already knows onto a record, so the alarm makes sense. Example: a login from another country is just a number on a screen. After enrichment it says — "this user is always in Chennai, this address is a known attacker, and this account can delete every file in the company."

What is correlation, in the simplest words?

Putting many small events side by side and noticing the pattern they form together.
One wrong password = a forgetful person.
Twenty wrong passwords in one minute = an attack.
The events are identical. Only the pattern is different — and a computer cannot see the pattern by itself, so somebody has to tell it the rule. That telling is correlation.

What is an alert?

The clear message the SIEM sends when it finds a pattern worth worrying about. Instead of sending 4,000 raw lines, it sends one message like "22 wrong passwords for the admin account in 4 minutes, from a known attacker — block it."

What is a false alarm, and why is it a problem?

A false alarm (false positive) looks dangerous but is harmless — for example, the nightly backup job looks like "thousands of files changed". The problem is quantity: many companies get 5,000 to 50,000 alarms a day and only 1 to 5 are real. The team starts ignoring alarms, and then they miss the one that really mattered. Too much noise is dangerous in itself.

What is a SOAR, and is it the same as a SIEM?

No. SIEM = the eye. It spots the problem and raises the alarm. SOAR = the hand. It carries out the fix — blocks the address, locks the account, creates a ticket — and can even do it by itself, at 3 a.m., within seconds of the alarm. They are two different tools that work together.

Can a computer be deleted so the attacker cannot see the log?

This is one of the most important reasons for a SIEM. If an attacker breaks in, he may try to erase his own trail from the computer. That is why the SIEM keeps its copy somewhere else and receives every record within seconds. Deleting the diary no longer works, because the master diary is safe. The SOC also sets an alarm for exactly this — "event log was cleared" is treated as a very serious attack.

What is a log source matrix?

A simple table that lists every computer and system that should send records to the SIEM, and who is responsible for it. It answers two questions the SOC must never get wrong: "what should be logging?" and "what has stopped logging?" A column for the "last checked date" turns log health into a checklist instead of a guess.

What is MTTD and MTTR?

MTTD = Mean Time To Detect — how long from the attack starting until the SOC noticed. MTTR = Mean Time To Respond or Recover — how long until it was sorted out.

Both should go down, not up. If MTTD is 3 months, your logs are not working properly.

Give me three things that make log collection go wrong.
  • Silent gaps — a computer stops sending and nobody notices for weeks. Fix: send a test ping every hour and alarm if it does not arrive.
  • Clocks not matching — if one computer's clock says 2:00 and another's says 1:40, you cannot prove what happened first. Fix: set all clocks automatically (NTP).
  • Collecting the wrong things — thousands of unimportant messages while the important ones are missing.
In one sentence, what is the job of a SOC?

To watch every computer in the company all day and night, notice when somebody is trying to break in, and stop them before any harm is done.

In one sentence, what does the SIEM do?

It gathers all the computer records in one place, puts them in one common language, spots the patterns that show an attack, and tells the SOC in one clear message what to do.

6. Quick Quiz

Check yourself. Click a question to see the answer.

Q1. In one word, what does a SOC's Level 2 person do that Level 1 does not?

Investigates. L1 filters alarms; L2 solves the hard real cases.

Q2. Name the five steps of a SIEM.

Collect → Make them the same → Add information → Find patterns → Raise one clear alarm.

Q3. A user fails his password 22 times in 4 minutes. Is that an attack on its own?

No, not on its own — but a very strong sign of one. This is exactly what correlation is for: deciding that the pattern is more likely an attack than forgetfulness.

Q4. Why does the SIEM copy logs to a central place instead of reading them on each computer?

Three reasons: one person cannot read thousands of records; the records are scattered across many computers; and if an attacker breaks in, he can erase his own diary on his own computer. A central copy is safe from all three problems.

Q5. What is the difference between a SIEM and a SOAR?

SIEM is the eye — it spots the problem and raises the alarm. SOAR is the hand — it performs the response automatically (block, lock, ticket).

Q6. Why are attacks often noticed during night shifts and weekends?

Because attackers know fewer people are watching then, and the company's response is slowest. This is why a SOC must work 24×7 across four shifts.
